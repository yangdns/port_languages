Background and meaning

Chang Hen Ge (《长恨歌》; Cháng Hèn Gē, “Song of Everlasting Sorrow”) is a long narrative poem by Bai Juyi(（白居易; Bái Jū Yì, 772-846), written in 806 under the Tang (唐; Táng) dynasty. It tells of the love between Emperor Xuanzong (玄宗; Xuán Zōng) and his consort Yang Guifei (杨贵妃; Yáng Guì Fēi), and of how it ended in catastrophe. The title line of this chorus comes from the poem’s turning point: the drums of war arrive and shatter the court’s music and dancing.

Those drums belong to the An Lushan (安禄山; Ān Lù Shān) Rebellion. In the winter of 755, An Lushan, military governor of the northeastern frontier, rose in revolt, later joined by Shi Siming (史思明, Shǐ Sīmíng). The war lasted eight years and left the Tang empire permanently weakened. The rebels took the eastern capital, Luoyang (洛阳); in the summer of 756 the fortress pass of Tongguan (潼关) fell, and the emperor fled toward Sichuan. At Mawei (马嵬), on the road west, his guards demanded Yang Guifei’s death.

The cantata was composed by Huang Zi (黄自; Huáng Zì, 1904-1938), a Chinese composer who studied in the United States, to a libretto by Wei Han Zhang (韦瀚章; Wéi Hàn Zhāng, 1906-1993) drawn from Bai Juyi’s poem. Written in the early 1930s, it is a landmark of Chinese choral music. This chorus is the moment the war breaks in.

In the poem the drum is the pi (鼙), a small drum carried by cavalry. The libretto shortens it to gu (鼓), “drum”.

The recommended musical expression might be Marziale (勇武的).

Rehearsal notes

How to read a column. Each column is one syllable. From the top: pinyin, simplified character, traditional character, Wade–Giles. A bar above a group of columns marks one word, to be sung as a unit; it matches the hyphens in the romanization.

Speed buttons. Every line has its own speed buttons (0.5× to 1×). Tapping one plays that line at that speed. Start slow, then step up until you can match the recording.

Aspiration. In Wade–Giles an apostrophe marks an aspirated consonant: t'a, p'o and ch'ang carry a puff of air, while ta, po and chang do not.

The vowel ü. In Yü- and chü-, round the lips as for “oo” and say “ee”, as in German ü or French u.

Wade–Giles to pinyin. hsi = xi, ch'i = qi, chih = zhi, chih = zhi, jih = ri, tsen = zen, ts'an = can, t'ung = tong.

Final consonants. Many lines end on -n (guān, ān, fàn, cán, hàn, shān). Close each one with the tongue tip against the ridge behind the upper teeth. Keep -ng (gōng, wàng, tóng) at the back of the tongue.

The reading voice speaks the words with their spoken Mandarin tones. Use it to check sounds and syllable length, not melody. In singing, the notes carry the pitch.
